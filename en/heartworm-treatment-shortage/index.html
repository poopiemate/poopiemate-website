Heartworm treatment is getting hard to find

Dog owners in Korea are talking about one thing this week: the heartworm treatment drug may stop coming in. People are especially worried about shelter dogs and dogs that live outdoors. Here is what we know so far, and what you can do now.

01What's happening?

  • Abroad: Veterinarians have discussed the upcoming withdrawal of Immiticide (melarsomine), the injection that kills adult heartworms, from markets outside the US. (Vet Sessions, Jan 19, 2026)
  • In Korea: Word is spreading that it will be hard to get from next year. There is no official announcement on timing yet, so ask your vet for the latest.

02Prevention is not affected

The concern is the treatment injection for dogs that are already infected. Monthly chewables and spot-ons, and preventive injections, are not part of this news.

TypeWhat it doesThis news
Monthly chewable or spot-onKills young larvae each month before they growNot affected; Korean-made options exist too
Preventive injectionProtects for months up to a yearNot affected
Treatment injection (Immiticide)Kills adult heartwormsBeing withdrawn outside the US; no substitute in Korea

03Is there a substitute?

Melarsomine is the only drug that directly kills adult heartworms. No company makes it in Korea, so it has always been imported. The US also has a generic version, but it is approved only there and can't simply be used in Korea. (dvm360) If supply stops, Korea has nothing to replace it.

04Why is supply ending outside the US?

The maker hasn't given an official reason. People point to falling demand as prevention spreads, and to the fact that it is an arsenic-based drug that is hard to make and register. It has run short several times before because of production problems. (dvm360)

05How are dogs treated without it?

Countries without melarsomine use a long course of an antibiotic together with a preventive. (Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2021) The worms die slowly, which can take months to more than a year, and the heart and lungs stay under strain the whole time, so exercise has to stay restricted. Always decide on treatment with your vet.

Good to know

Cats have no approved heartworm treatment at all. For cats, prevention is the only option.

06Why shelter and outdoor dogs worry people most

WhoWhyWhat to do
Indoor dogsBitten on walks or by mosquitoes that get insideMonthly prevention; test first after a long gap

07What you can do now

  • Never skip prevention: many vets recommend year-round prevention. Set the plan for your area with your vet.
  • Test after a gap: if you are starting for the first time or skipped a few months, get tested first.
  • Ask for test results when adopting: always ask the shelter about a heartworm test.
  • Outdoor dogs too: dogs in yards, farms and shops need prevention as well.
  • Help shelters: donating preventives is one of the most useful things right now. Ask the shelter what they accept first.

08Questions to ask your vet

  • Do you still have the treatment injection in stock?
  • If supply stops, how would you treat an infected dog?
  • Does my dog need year-round prevention?

09Don't miss a dose

Once a month is easy to forget. In POOPIEMATE you can log meds and turn on medicine reminders, and the whole family sees who gave the last dose, so nobody doubles up or skips one.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Will heartworm preventives run out too?

No. This news is about the treatment injection for dogs that are already infected.
Monthly preventives and preventive injections are not affected.

When will it run out in Korea?

There is no official announcement yet.
The withdrawal outside the US has been discussed, and word in Korea is that it gets hard to find from next year.
Ask your vet about stock and timing.

Can an infected dog still be treated without it?

Yes, but it is slower.
Countries without melarsomine use a long course of an antibiotic plus a preventive.
It takes much longer and the heart and lungs stay under strain, so decide with your vet.

Do dogs need prevention in winter too?

Many vets recommend year-round prevention, and it matters more as treatment gets harder.
Set the plan for your area with your vet.
